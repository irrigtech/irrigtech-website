Smart Irrigation Valve Controllers: How They Work and How to Choose One

Schedule, weather or soil moisture: how smart valve controllers decide when to water, and the seven things to check before you buy one.

Smart Irrigation · 24 Nov 2026 · 7 min read

A smart irrigation valve controller – also sold as a smart control valve, smart water valve or IoT valve controller – opens and closes irrigation valves automatically and reports back what it did. Unlike a plain timer, it can water because the soil is dry and skip a cycle because it isn't.

Three ways a controller decides

  • Schedule: fixed days and run times – simple, but blind to rain and heat.
  • Weather: forecasts or a local weather station shorten, extend or skip cycles.
  • Soil moisture: a sensor in the root zone triggers irrigation when moisture drops below a threshold you set for that zone.

The best results usually come from combining them: soil moisture decides whether to water, the schedule decides when watering is allowed, and the weather can veto a cycle before rain.

Seven things to check before you buy

  1. Valve type and voltage. Many irrigation solenoid valves run on 24 VAC from a mains-powered controller; battery-powered controllers usually need DC latching solenoids. Match the controller to the valves you have.
  2. Number of zones. One output per zone, plus room to grow.
  3. Connectivity. WiFi works near buildings; for valves out in the field, long-range radio such as LoRa or cellular is more reliable.
  4. Power. Mains, battery or solar – and how long a battery lasts at your watering frequency.
  5. Feedback. Does it confirm that each valve actually opened and closed? Flow or pressure feedback catches stuck valves and leaks.
  6. Fail-safe and manual override. What happens if the connection drops mid-cycle? A good controller closes valves after a maximum run time and can still be operated by hand.
  7. Sensor integration. Can it use soil moisture readings directly, per zone, rather than one sensor for the whole farm?

Drip lines, sprinklers and greenhouses

The same controller logic works for drip lines, smart sprinkler valves and greenhouse zones. Drip zones typically run longer and more often; sprinkler zones need attention to wind and evaporation; greenhouse zones benefit most from substrate moisture sensors.

Installation tips

  • Group plants with similar water needs into the same zone.
  • Place the soil moisture sensor where it represents the zone, not directly under an emitter.
  • Test every zone manually before switching to automatic mode.
